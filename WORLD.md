# The Lost & Found

## What this place is

The Lost & Found is a building that holds every object anyone has ever set
down and not picked back up. It has no outside — no windows onto a street, no
weather, no sky. It is built of corridors that give onto rooms that give onto
more corridors, and each room holds one kind of arrival: a room of umbrellas
propped in a slowly leaning row, a room of keys on hooks numbered past any
lock that still exists, a room where coats hang so dense the aisle narrows to
a shoulder's width. Nobody staffs it. Nobody built it, as far as anyone can
tell. It keeps its own inventory, in ledgers that appear to write themselves.

## Rules of the world

- Objects here are the real objects, unaltered: brand names, dents, stains,
  and smells intact. The building does not clean, restore, or symbolize them.
- The building does not manufacture objects. Everything in it arrived because
  someone, somewhere else, put it down and walked away.
- No one is trapped and nobody is searching for a way out. A visitor here is
  browsing, the way you'd browse a very large secondhand shop — not fleeing.
- The building is indifferent, never cruel, never tender on purpose. It has
  no opinion of the visitor. Nothing here is hunting, watching, or baiting.
- There is no day or night, no season, no clock anywhere, and nothing decays
  on a schedule. An object can be freshly arrived and already dusty; that is
  not a contradiction to resolve.
- No other people ever appear on stage. Their absence is felt only through
  what they left: a shoe with the lace still tied, a paperback with a receipt
  used as a bookmark, a child's drawing with a name half rubbed out.
- Rooms connect to rooms; there is no second floor and no basement. The
  building does not have a layout that could be drawn as a map, and a passage
  should never try to make it one.
- Light comes from somewhere unspecified — lamps that are always lit,
  skylights with no sky behind them. Do not explain the light source and do
  not draw attention to the fact that it has none.
- An object a visitor leaves behind takes its place among the others at once
  and without ceremony. The building does not react, reward, or comment.
- Nothing here is dangerous. No violence, no threat of harm, no gore. Unease
  comes from scale, repetition, and the ordinariness of what is lost — never
  from menace.

## Voice

Second person, present tense, throughout — "you" is always the visitor,
acting right now. Sentences run short to medium, mostly declarative, built
around one or two clauses. One precise noun beats three vague adjectives. No
exclamation marks. No rhetorical questions. Never address the reader as "dear
reader," a "player," or a "you" outside the fiction, and never acknowledge
that this is a story, a game, or generated. Commas over semicolons. A short
sentence closes a passage better than a long one.

The register is a museum placard written by a cataloguer who has spent too
many years alone with the collection and started to care about it more than
is strictly professional.

## Vocabulary to prefer

corridor, vestibule, shelf, ledger, hook, crate, drawer, lamp, doorway, sill,
alcove, satchel, tag, label, stub, latch, threshold, cabinet, trunk, glove,
umbrella, spectacles, ticket, buckle, hinge, rail, hatbox, receipt, dust,
wool

## Vocabulary to avoid

magic, portal, realm, destiny, journey, adventure, hero, quest, whisper,
ancient, mystical, shimmer, veil, secrets, darkness (as a stand-in for evil),
forgotten (as a blanket adjective for the whole building), eerie, sentient,
haunted, spirit, echo (as a verb for memory)
